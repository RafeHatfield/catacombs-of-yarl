**Q1: WHAT IS THIS FLOOR MADE OF?**
Dressed stone slabs — cut rectangular flagging, sandy-brown, laid tight with thin joints. Not brick, not tile, not dirt. Something like a sandstone or limestone paving.

**Q1_WHY:** The rectangles are *cut sizes*, not modules — at x460–620/y40–150 a single slab runs ~85px wide and ~28px tall, sitting beside squares of ~30×30 and a tall ~24×55. That size spread is stonecutting, not manufacture. Joints are thin dark lines, not raked mortar beds. And the cracks at x415/y16 running south, and the arc across x280–620/y300–388, propagate *across* slab boundaries and change direction without stopping — stone fails like that, wood and tile don't.

**Q2: WHICH WAY WOULD YOU WALK, AND WHY?**
West, toward the opening at x0–188 / y145–265.

Nothing about the ground drove that. I picked it because it's a two-tile-wide opening versus a one-tile corridor south, and because it's closer. I checked the ground in front of both before answering and it is the same ground: same slab sizes, same mark density, same hue. The floor did not vote.

**Q3: HAS ANYTHING HAPPENED TO THIS FLOOR?**
It has been cracked and it has been scratched, and that is the whole list. A real crack network runs through it — a main fracture entering the north wall at x≈415 and travelling south-southwest past the figure to about x355/y250, then on down to the bottom edge; a second from the west wall at (188,205) angling down-right; a branching set of arcs through the lower third. Those are good. Over the rest of it, every slab carries the same handful of scratch motifs at the same density.

What has *not* happened to it: nothing has been spilled on it, nothing has been dragged across it, nothing has been repaired, nothing has been worn. There is no dirt. For a floor in an administered underworld under four hundred years of orc garrison and however many centuries before that, it is remarkably clean.

**Q3_WHY:** Detail density measured per 20px cell across the normalised floor sits in a band of roughly 0.08–0.18 across essentially the entire room — the only cells that fall outside are the sprite and the blank slabs listed in Q4. There is no contiguous low-detail region (polish) and no contiguous high-detail region (accumulation). Six flat dark slabs read at first glance as stains or pits, but magnified at (530,200)–(624,236) the rectangle has hard edges exactly on the slab bounds, no rim, no shadow, no falloff. It is not a stain and not a hole. It is a slab with the texture layer left off.

**Q4: IS THERE ANYTHING YOUR EYE KEEPS RETURNING TO?**
Two things.

**The blank slabs.** Six of them: (285,128)–(345,152), (370,120)–(414,145), (500,86)–(556,116), (455,190)–(508,216), (530,200)–(624,236), (200,290)–(276,325). The one at (455,190)–(508,216) sits immediately right of the figure and competes with the character sprite at 1:1.

**The "/" hatch.** A set of parallel 45° scratches running bottom-left to top-right. Once you see it you cannot stop seeing it — (470,50)–(556,80), (555,120)–(600,150), (490,240)–(540,270), (420,300)–(470,330), (240,60)–(300,110), and dozens more. It is on the majority of marked slabs, at the same angle, at the same spacing, in roughly the same quantity. It stops being scratches and becomes a screen door.

**Q5: MADE, OR REPEATED?**
**Made** — but only just, and it's the layout doing the work, not the surface.

The slab arrangement is genuinely non-repeating. I ran autocorrelation on the flattened floor and there is no periodic peak at any lag from 1 to 60px; the correlation decays monotonically. I also block-matched at every plausible tile size (24/28/30/31/32/36px) and found no exact duplicate anywhere. Nobody stamped a tileset here.

What saves it is the crack network. Cracks are the one element that knows about the room as a whole rather than about individual slabs.

What undercuts it is the mark vocabulary. There are four motifs — the 45° hatch, rows of short horizontal dashes, vertical tally strokes of three to six, and blank. Roughly 150 slabs, four motifs, randomised offset. It isn't a repeated *image*, but it is a repeated *decision*, and at a glance those feel the same.

**Q6: WOULD YOU BELIEVE THIS SHIPPED IN A COMMERCIAL GAME?**
**YES.**

Merely competent, or genuinely good? **Merely competent.** The layout is above average and the cracks are properly good. The surface is not. A floor that is genuinely good tells you something you didn't know before you looked at it; this one tells you "stone, old" and stops.

**Q8: HOW OLD IS THIS FLOOR?**
**One age everywhere — a few centuries. Old, but not older than the records.** And that uniformity is the problem, because this place should be a palimpsest and it reads as a single pour.

**Q8_WHY:** The cracking at x415/y16→x355/y250 and the arcs at x280–620/y300–388 are mature — they've propagated across many slabs, which takes long settlement. That's the "centuries" read. But nothing anywhere reads *older* or *newer* than anything else: no slab is more broken than its neighbour, no section is replacement stone, no patch is fresher. I checked (200,290)–(276,325) in the south-west against (500,86)–(556,116) in the north-east — opposite corners of the room, and their mark density and mark type are indistinguishable.

Most telling: there is no repair. The fiction says a garrison has been holding and mending this place for four hundred years with rope, pins, hide and salvaged timber. Not one slab here has been lifted, replaced, shimmed, pinned or planked over. A floor under that kind of custody would be a patchwork of ages. This one has no seams of history in it at all — which, read straight, means it was laid once and abandoned, not administered.

**Q10: WHICH WAY IS USED MOST, WHICH LEAST?**
**They look the same.**

In front of the west opening — x188–250, y150–265 — the slabs are the same size, the same hue, carry the same hatch, at the same density as the room's interior. In front of the south corridor mouth — x360–450, y340–388 — identical. Neither threshold is darkened, polished, chipped, dished, or scuffed. Neither has debris pushed to its sides.

A one-tile corridor south is a choke point; four hundred years of a garrison moving through it would have dished the stone in the middle of that opening and left the corners of the room untouched. Nothing distinguishes them. The floor has no opinion about its own doors.
