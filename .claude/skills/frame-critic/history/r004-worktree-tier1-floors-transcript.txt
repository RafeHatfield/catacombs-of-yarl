**BEST:** 2

**BEST_WHY:** The masonry in the lit area is the only genuinely authored surface in the set. In the row running through the figure's feet, the slab widths change along a single course — a wide slab left of the sprite, two narrow ones under it, a wide one right — and the coursing doesn't reset to a running bond, so the floor reads as stone that was cut and fitted rather than a repeating stamp. The joints hold a consistent dark value at consistent width while the slab faces vary, which is what separates cut stone from a grid overlay. Above and left of the figure there are pale diagonal streaks raking across several slabs at once, crossing joints without breaking — that's the lamp grazing an uneven floor, and it's drawn as one continuous event across multiple tiles rather than per-tile. There's a cast shadow below-left of the sprite that darkens the slab faces but not the joints. And in the upper-right, at the edge of the falloff, the slabs are still legible as individual blocks at very low value — I sampled a lit row and found 34 distinct luminance values across 140 pixels with hard steps at the joints. Holding block separation at that value range is the hard part, and it's done.

**FLIP 2:**
- Remove the magenta fill entirely. The out-of-sight tiles need to be rendered in the same stone palette, darkened — not substituted with a different hue.
- Remove the per-tile corner swatches. Every one of them is a data readout, and they sit at the same value as the lit floor so they read as glowing chips embedded in the ground.
- The dark region upper-left and the magenta region are two different systems for the same state. Pick one falloff curve and run the whole frame off it, so the left edge of the light pool has the same character as the top edge.
- The bottom edge of the light pool cuts off on a hard tile boundary at the row below the figure — the last lit row is at full value and the next row is out. Ramp it across at least two tiles like the right-hand edge already does.
- The floor is uniform stone slab across everything visible. Four hundred years of orc repair is the stated content and none of it is present: no driven pins, no roped-together slabs, no salvaged timber patch, no hide. Put actual repairs into the surface — the coursing quality is good enough to carry them.

**FLIP 1:**
- Give the pond an edge. Replace the straight tile-aligned boundary with an irregular one, add a darker band of one to two pixels where the water meets the bank, and shadow the dirt on the side the light comes from.
- The pond interior is two flat colours across its whole area. Add depth variation — darker toward the centre or wherever it's deep — and break up the uniform honeycomb tiling, which currently repeats identically end to end.
- The green blobs used as algae in the water are the same asset used as moss on the grey stone at the right edge, identical shape and palette. Draw the submerged ones separately; a thing under water does not have the same edge as a thing on dry stone.
- The frame has no light falloff — the four corners are within 2.7 luminance of each other. Every other frame in the set is lit by the figure's lamp. Either apply that falloff here or this frame is from a different game.
- The barrels at the bottom are drawn in elevation: elliptical lid plus a visible cylindrical side wall running downward, lit from the upper left. The crates at the top left show their front faces. They are side-view props sitting on a straight-down floor. Redraw both as plan views — lid and a thin rim only.
- The grey floor is dead flat. Every tile is the same value with the same speckle sprinkle. Vary the slab faces, and vary the joint depth along a run so the stones read as cut rather than as a grid.
- The hairline crack on the grey tile at the right edge stops dead at the tile boundary and does not continue into the tile below it. Carry cracks across tile seams or delete them.
- The white scatter in the centre-right of the dirt has no silhouette — it reads as noise, not as bones or debris. Give it a recognisable shape or remove it.
- The wall torch on the left is a stack of orange rectangles above a brown stick, seen from the side, and it throws no light on the bricks either side of it. From directly above a torch is a bright point in a bracket, not a flame drawn in elevation.

**FLIP 3:**
- Build a wall that reads. Give wall tiles a distinct top surface value clearly separated from the lit floor, add visible thickness at the edge, and cast a shadow from the wall onto the floor on the far side from the lamp. Right now the passage below the figure has no sides.
- Sharpen the floor. The slab edges ramp over two to three pixels and adjacent blocks merge until the joint vanishes; step them at a hard boundary so the floor sits at the same resolution as the sprite.
- Take the cracks off the top of the lighting. They are pure black inside the brightest part of the lamp pool, which makes them the darkest thing in the frame; they need to be lit along with the surface they're in.
- Deflect the cracks at joints. A crack that crosses six slabs in one smooth curve without registering a single joint reads as a line drawn over the floor, not damage in it. Break them at joints, or run them along one.
- Give the cracks a section — a lighter lip on the lamp-facing side, dark in the channel. A uniform 1px black stroke has no depth.
- Fix the horizontal seam where the slab floor meets the mottled brown region. Add a threshold, a lip, or scattered debris crossing the line so the two surfaces interlock rather than butt.
- The mottled brown region has no structure at any scale and no relationship to the slab grid. Give it a legible material — rubble at a stated size, packed earth with direction, something with an edge — or make it stone.
- Over half the canvas carries no information. Either raise the ambient floor so the far architecture is faintly legible, or tighten the frame so the picture isn't mostly empty black.
- Same note as the other frame: the stated content is four hundred years of continuous repair with rope, pins, hide and salvaged timber. There is none of it in the floor. The falloff is the only thing doing work here.

If that's the case, the fix is one job, not two: take the crisper masonry and the falloff off the second and put them together.
